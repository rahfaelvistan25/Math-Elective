Section I

Introduction to the Module

Mathematics is an important tool in business. Business owners and managers use mathematical models to understand how much it costs to produce products, how much money they can earn, how much profit they can make, and how changes in prices and production affect their businesses.

Calculus for Business helps us understand these relationships. In this module, we will study business functions and limits, which are important foundations for more advanced topics in calculus.

The Small Bakery Scenario

Imagine that you own a small bakery. You need to answer critical questions such as:

How much will it cost to produce 100 cakes?
How much money will you earn if you sell 100 cakes?
How many cakes should you sell to avoid losing money?
What happens to your average cost when you produce more cakes?
How does the selling price affect customer demand?
What happens to business costs when production approaches a certain level?

We can answer all of these questions using mathematical functions.

Learning Objectives

At the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

  • Explain what a mathematical function is and how it applies to business.
  • Identify fixed costs, variable costs, total costs, revenue, and profit.
  • Construct linear and nonlinear business functions.
  • Understand and solve basic demand and supply functions.
  • Calculate market equilibrium using demand and supply equations.
  • Understand break-even analysis and calculate the break-even point.
  • Evaluate limits using substitution, factoring, and other basic methods.
  • Explain the meaning of limits in real-life business situations.
  • Determine whether a business function is continuous or discontinuous.
  • Solve business-related problems involving functions, limits, and continuity.
Part 1

Understanding Business Functions

1. What is a function?

A function is a mathematical relationship in which one quantity depends on another quantity.

In simpler words, a function tells us how one value changes when another value changes.

Simple example

Suppose a school supplies store sells notebooks for ₱25 each. The total sales depend on the number of notebooks sold.

Number of notebooks (\(x\)) Total sales (\(R(x)\))
1₱25
2₱50
3₱75
4₱100
5₱125

Notice that the total sales increase as the number of notebooks sold increases.

We can represent this relationship using a function:

\[ R(x)=25x \]

Where:

  • \(R(x)\) = total revenue or sales
  • \(x\) = number of notebooks sold
  • \(25\) = selling price of each notebook
Step-by-step example

School Supplies Store Sales

Question: How much revenue will the store earn if it sells 12 notebooks?

Step 1
Write the function: \[ R(x)=25x \]
Step 2
Identify the given value: The store sells 12 notebooks, so: \[ x=12 \]
Step 3
Substitute 12 into the function: \[ R(12)=25(12) \]
Step 4
Multiply: \[ R(12)=300 \]
Final Answer:

The store will earn ₱300 from selling 12 notebooks.

Remember: A function allows a business to predict an outcome based on a given quantity.

2. Important symbols used in business functions

Before studying business functions, understand these essential symbols:

Symbol Meaning Example
\(x\) or \(q\) Quantity or number of products 100 products
\(P\) Price per unit or selling price ₱50
\(C(x)\) Total cost function Total production expenses
\(R(x)\) Revenue function Total sales income
\(\pi(x)\) Profit function Money earned after costs
\(F\) Fixed cost Rent
\(v\) Variable cost per unit Materials per product
\(D\) Demand Quantity customers want to buy
\(S\) Supply Quantity sellers offer

Note: For the examples in this lesson, we will usually use \(x\) or \(q\) to represent the number of products.

Part 2

Cost Functions

3. What is a cost function?

A cost function represents the total amount of money a business spends to produce goods or provide services.

For example, a bakery spends money on:

Flour, sugar, eggs & ingredients Electricity and water Employee salaries Equipment Rent Packaging materials

These expenses are part of the business's costs. There are two important types of costs:

A. Fixed costs (\(F\))

Fixed costs are expenses that generally stay the same even when the business produces more or fewer products, within a relevant operating range.

  • Monthly rent
  • Business permits
  • Insurance
  • Equipment rental
  • Fixed monthly salaries
Example: A bakery pays ₱10,000 monthly rent. Whether it produces 100 cakes or 500 cakes, the rent remains ₱10,000 for that month.
\[ F=₱10,000 \]

B. Variable costs (\(VC\))

Variable costs change depending on how many products a business produces.

  • Raw materials
  • Packaging
  • Ingredients
  • Production supplies
  • Per-unit manufacturing expenses
Example: Bakery spends ₱30 on ingredients & packaging for every cake:
  • 1 cake = ₱30
  • 2 cakes = ₱60
  • 10 cakes = ₱300
  • 100 cakes = ₱3,000
\[ VC(x)=vx \]
\(VC(x)\) = total variable cost, \(v\) = variable cost per unit, \(x\) = units produced

4. Linear cost functions

A linear cost function is a cost relationship that increases at a constant rate. This means that every additional product adds the same amount to total cost.

\[ \boxed{C(x)=F+vx} \]
\(C(x)\) = total cost • \(F\) = fixed cost • \(v\) = variable cost per unit • \(x\) = quantity produced
Worked Example 1

Bakery Production Cost

A bakery has the following expenses:

  • Monthly rent: ₱10,000
  • Ingredients and packaging per cake: ₱30
  • Number of cakes produced: \(x\)

Question: Construct the cost function and determine the total cost of producing 100 cakes.

Step 1
Identify the fixed cost: The monthly rent does not change with the number of cakes. \[ F=10,000 \]
Step 2
Identify the variable cost per unit: Each cake costs ₱30 to produce. \[ v=30 \]
Step 3
Write the linear cost function: \[ C(x)=F+vx \] Substitute the values: \[ \boxed{C(x)=10,000+30x} \]
Step 4
Substitute 100 for \(x\): \[ C(100)=10,000+30(100) \]
Step 5
Multiply & Add: \[ C(100)=10,000+3,000 \] \[ C(100)=13,000 \]
Final Answer:

The total cost of producing 100 cakes is ₱13,000.

Understanding the result

The ₱13,000 total cost consists of:

ExpenseAmount
Fixed cost (rent)₱10,000
Variable cost (ingredients and packaging: \(30 \times 100\))₱3,000
Total cost₱13,000

Important: Fixed cost remains constant, but variable cost increases as production increases.

Another Example

Printing Business

A printing shop pays ₱5,000 monthly rent. Printing each shirt costs ₱80 in materials and labor.

Its cost function is: \[ C(x)=5,000+80x \]

Find the cost of producing 50 shirts:

\[ C(50)=5,000+80(50) \] \[ C(50)=5,000+4,000 \] \[ \boxed{C(50)=₱9,000} \]

The total production cost is ₱9,000.

5. Nonlinear cost functions

A nonlinear cost function is a cost relationship that does not increase at a constant rate. In a nonlinear model, the cost of producing additional products can change as production increases.

Why does cost become nonlinear in business?
  • Machines become less efficient when overused.
  • Workers need overtime pay.
  • Additional equipment is required.
  • The business receives bulk discounts on materials.
  • Production becomes more complicated at higher quantities.

One common nonlinear cost function is quadratic:

\[ \boxed{C(x)=F+ax+bx^2} \]

For this example, \(a\) represents a basic variable cost and \(bx^2\) represents an additional production-related cost.

Worked Example 2

Growing Production Costs (Quadratic Cost)

A furniture company has:

  • Fixed cost: ₱2,000
  • Basic variable cost: ₱20 per table
  • Additional production cost represented by \(0.05x^2\)

Its cost function is: \[ C(x)=2,000+20x+0.05x^2 \]

Question: Find the total cost of producing 20 tables.

Step 1
Write the function: \[ C(x)=2,000+20x+0.05x^2 \]
Step 2
Substitute \(x=20\): \[ C(20)=2,000+20(20)+0.05(20)^2 \]
Step 3
Solve multiplication and exponent: \[ C(20)=2,000+400+0.05(400) \]
Step 4
Multiply: \[ C(20)=2,000+400+20 \]
Step 5
Add: \[ \boxed{C(20)=₱2,420} \]
Final Answer:

The total cost of producing 20 tables is ₱2,420.

What makes this function nonlinear?

The term \(x^2\) makes the function nonlinear. The total cost does not increase by the same amount for every additional table. The additional production-related cost becomes larger as production increases.

Linear vs. nonlinear cost functions

Feature Linear Cost Nonlinear Cost
Formula \(C(x)=F+vx\) \(C(x)=F+ax+bx^2\)
Rate of Increase Constant increase per unit Rate of increase can change
Graphical Shape Straight-line graph Curved graph
Business Context Simple production model More flexible production model (overtime, wear, bulk tiers)
Part 3

Revenue Functions

6. What is revenue?

Revenue is the total amount of money a business receives from selling products or services before deducting expenses.

For example, if a store sells 20 bags for ₱500 each, its revenue is: \[ 20(500)=₱10,000 \]
Revenue is not the same as profit!

A business may have high revenue but low profit (or even a loss) if its expenses are also high.

7. Linear revenue functions

A linear revenue function applies when the selling price per unit remains constant.

\[ \boxed{R(x)=px} \]
\(R(x)\) = total revenue • \(p\) = selling price per unit • \(x\) = number of units sold
Worked Example 3

Clothing Store Revenue

A clothing store sells T-shirts for ₱250 each.

Question: How much revenue will the store earn if it sells 40 T-shirts?

Step 1
Identify the selling price: \[ p=250 \]
Step 2
Identify the number of products sold: \[ x=40 \]
Step 3
Write the revenue function: \[ R(x)=250x \]
Step 4
Substitute 40: \[ R(40)=250(40) \]
Step 5
Multiply: \[ \boxed{R(40)=₱10,000} \]
Final Answer:

The store earns ₱10,000 in revenue.

Another Example

Water Bottle Business

A business sells water bottles for ₱35 each. Its revenue function is: \[ R(x)=35x \]

Find the revenue from selling 150 bottles:

\[ R(150)=35(150) \] \[ \boxed{R(150)=₱5,250} \]

8. Nonlinear revenue functions

Revenue becomes nonlinear when the selling price changes depending on the number of products sold.

Discounts for bulk purchases Promotional pricing Changes in customer demand Competition Price increases or decreases

Suppose a business finds that it needs to lower its price to sell more products. Its selling price may be represented by: \[ p(x)=120-x \] This means the price per unit depends on the quantity sold.

The revenue function is: \[ R(x)=x \times p(x) \]

Substitute the selling-price function and expand:

\[ R(x)=x(120-x) \] \[ \boxed{R(x)=120x-x^2} \]

This is a nonlinear revenue function because it contains \(x^2\).

Worked Example 4

Nonlinear Revenue Calculation

A business uses the following selling-price model: \[ p(x)=120-x \]

Question: Find the total revenue if the business sells 50 products.

Step 1
Write the price function: \[ p(x)=120-x \]
Step 2
Substitute 50: \[ p(50)=120-50 = 70 \] The selling price is ₱70 per product.
Step 3
Calculate revenue: \[ R(x)=xp(x) \] \[ R(50)=50(70) = \boxed{₱3,500} \]
Final Answer:

The business earns ₱3,500.

Understanding the nonlinear relationship

Products sold (\(x\)) Price per product (\(p(x)\)) Total revenue (\(R(x)\))
20₱100₱2,000
40₱80₱3,200
50₱70₱3,500
60 (Peak)₱60₱3,600 (Maximum)
100₱20₱2,000
Notice that selling more products does not always mean earning more revenue!

The business may need to reduce its selling price to attract additional customers. Eventually, the lower price can outweigh the benefit of selling more units.

Important difference:

  • Linear revenue: Every additional unit sold produces the same additional revenue when the price is constant.
  • Nonlinear revenue: Revenue changes at a different rate because the selling price depends on quantity.
Part 4

Profit Functions

9. What is profit?

Profit is the amount of money remaining after subtracting the total cost from total revenue.

Revenue (Money in)
Cost (Money spent)
Profit (What remains)
\[ \boxed{\pi(x)=R(x)-C(x)} \]
\(\pi(x)\) = profit • \(R(x)\) = revenue • \(C(x)\) = total cost

There are three possible results:

Result Condition Business Meaning
Profit is positive (\(\pi(x) > 0\)) \(R(x) > C(x)\) The business earns money (Gain).
Profit is zero (\(\pi(x) = 0\)) \(R(x) = C(x)\) The business breaks even (No gain, no loss).
Profit is negative (\(\pi(x) < 0\)) \(R(x) < C(x)\) The business experiences a loss.

10. Linear profit functions

A linear profit function occurs when both revenue and cost are linear.

Worked Example 5

Small Bakery Profit

A bakery sells each cake for ₱100. Its production costs are:

  • Fixed cost: ₱2,000
  • Variable cost: ₱30 per cake

Question: Construct the profit function and determine the profit when the bakery sells 60 cakes.

Step 1
Write the revenue function: Selling price is ₱100 per cake. \[ R(x)=100x \]
Step 2
Write the cost function: \[ C(x)=2,000+30x \]
Step 3
Apply the profit formula: \[ \pi(x)=R(x)-C(x) \] Substitute both functions: \[ \pi(x)=100x-(2,000+30x) \]
Step 4
Remove the parentheses: (Distribute the negative sign!) \[ \pi(x)=100x-2,000-30x \]
Step 5
Combine similar terms: \[ \boxed{\pi(x)=70x-2,000} \] This is the bakery's linear profit function.
Step 6
Find the profit from selling 60 cakes: Substitute \(x=60\). \[ \pi(60)=70(60)-2,000 \] \[ \pi(60)=4,200-2,000 \] \[ \boxed{\pi(60)=₱2,200} \]
Final Answer:

The bakery earns ₱2,200 in profit after selling 60 cakes.

Check your understanding

Why is the profit function \(70x-2,000\) instead of \(100x-2,000\)?

Because the bakery earns ₱100 from each cake but spends ₱30 to produce each cake. The amount remaining from each sale before paying fixed costs is: \[ 100-30=₱70 \] That is why the variable part of the profit function is \(70x\). (This ₱70 is known in business as the contribution margin).

11. Nonlinear profit functions

Profit becomes nonlinear when revenue or cost, or both, are nonlinear.

Worked Example 6

Business with Changing Selling Prices

A company sells products using the following price function: \[ p(x)=120-x \]

Its total cost function is: \[ C(x)=1,600+20x \]

Question: Construct the profit function and find the profit when 50 products are sold.

Step 1
Find the revenue function: Revenue equals quantity multiplied by selling price. \[ R(x)=xp(x)=x(120-x)=120x-x^2 \]
Step 2
Write the cost function: \[ C(x)=1,600+20x \]
Step 3
Apply the profit formula: \[ \pi(x)=R(x)-C(x) \] \[ \pi(x)=(120x-x^2)-(1,600+20x) \]
Step 4
Remove the parentheses: \[ \pi(x)=120x-x^2-1,600-20x \]
Step 5
Combine similar terms: \[ \boxed{\pi(x)=-x^2+100x-1,600} \] This is a nonlinear profit function.
Step 6
Find the profit when 50 products are sold: \[ \pi(50)=-(50)^2+100(50)-1,600 \] \[ \pi(50)=-2,500+5,000-1,600 \] \[ \boxed{\pi(50)=₱900} \]
Final Answer:

The business earns ₱900 in profit.

Linear vs. nonlinear profit

Feature Linear Profit Nonlinear Profit
Form \(\pi(x)=mx+b\) Often contains \(x^2\) or higher powers (e.g. \(-ax^2+bx-c\))
Rate of Change Profit changes at a constant rate Profit changes at a varying rate
Occurrence Common when selling price and unit costs are constant Common when price or production costs change with quantity
Part 5

Demand and Supply Functions

12. What is demand?

Demand refers to the quantity of a product or service that customers are willing and able to buy at a given price during a particular period.

For example:
  • When the price of rice is low, customers may buy more rice.
  • When the price of rice is high, customers may buy less rice.

Law of demand

When the price increases, the quantity demanded generally decreases, assuming other factors remain unchanged.

When the price decreases, the quantity demanded generally increases.

13. Linear demand functions

A linear demand function describes a straight-line relationship between price and quantity.

\[ \boxed{Q_d=a-bP} \]
\(Q_d\) = quantity demanded • \(a\) = maximum quantity in the model • \(b\) = sensitivity (rate quantity drops per ₱1 price hike) • \(P\) = selling price
Worked Example 7

Demand for Milk Tea

A milk tea shop estimates that its daily demand follows: \[ Q_d=200-4P \] where \(P\) is the selling price in pesos.

Question: How many cups will customers demand if the selling price is ₱30?

Step 1
Write the demand function: \[ Q_d=200-4P \]
Step 2
Substitute the selling price \(P=30\): \[ Q_d=200-4(30) \]
Step 3
Multiply: \[ Q_d=200-120 \]
Step 4
Subtract: \[ \boxed{Q_d=80} \]
Final Answer:

Customers are expected to demand 80 cups per day.

What happens if the price changes?

Selling price (\(P\)) Calculation (\(200-4P\)) Quantity demanded (\(Q_d\))
₱10\(200 - 4(10) = 200 - 40\)160 cups
₱20\(200 - 4(20) = 200 - 80\)120 cups
₱30\(200 - 4(30) = 200 - 120\)80 cups
₱40\(200 - 4(40) = 200 - 160\)40 cups
₱50\(200 - 4(50) = 200 - 200\)0 cups

Notice the relationship: as the price increases, the quantity demanded decreases.

14. Nonlinear demand functions

A nonlinear demand function represents a curved relationship between price and quantity demanded.

\[ \boxed{P=120-0.01Q^2} \]
\(P\) = price • \(Q\) = quantity demanded
Worked Example 8

Nonlinear Demand for a Product

A company estimates that the market price associated with selling quantity \(Q\) is: \[ P=120-0.01Q^2 \]

Question: Determine the price when the quantity is 50 units.

Step 1
Write the function: \[ P=120-0.01Q^2 \]
Step 2
Substitute 50: \[ P=120-0.01(50)^2 \]
Step 3
Square 50: \[ P=120-0.01(2,500) \]
Step 4
Multiply & subtract: \[ P=120-25 \implies \boxed{P=₱95} \]
Final Answer:

The price associated with a quantity of 50 units is ₱95 per unit. This is nonlinear because the function contains \(Q^2\).

15. What is supply?

Supply refers to the quantity of a product or service that sellers are willing and able to offer for sale at a particular price during a given period.

For example, when the selling price of a product increases, businesses may be encouraged to produce and sell more because they have an opportunity to earn more revenue.

Law of supply

When the price increases, the quantity supplied generally increases, assuming other factors remain unchanged.

When the price decreases, the quantity supplied generally decreases.

16. Linear supply functions

A linear supply function describes a straight-line relationship between price and quantity supplied.

\[ \boxed{Q_s=c+dP} \]
\(Q_s\) = quantity supplied • \(c\) = starting quantity • \(d\) = rate supply changes per unit price increase • \(P\) = selling price
Worked Example 9

Supply of Bottled Water

A bottled water supplier uses the following supply function: \[ Q_s=20+3P \]

Question: How many bottles will the supplier offer when the price is ₱20?

Step 1
Write the function: \[ Q_s=20+3P \]
Step 2
Substitute \(P=20\): \[ Q_s=20+3(20) \]
Step 3
Multiply & Add: \[ Q_s=20+60 \implies \boxed{Q_s=80} \]
Final Answer:

The supplier will offer 80 bottles.

17. Nonlinear supply functions

A nonlinear supply function describes a curved relationship between price and quantity supplied.

\[ \boxed{P=20+0.005Q^2} \]

The squared term shows that price increases at a changing rate as the supplier offers more products.

Worked Example 10

Nonlinear Supply Calculation

A supplier uses the function: \[ P=20+0.005Q^2 \]

Question: Find the required price when the supplier offers 40 units.

Step 1
Write the function: \[ P=20+0.005Q^2 \]
Step 2
Substitute \(Q=40\): \[ P=20+0.005(40)^2 \]
Step 3
Square 40: \[ P=20+0.005(1,600) \]
Step 4
Multiply & add: \[ P=20+8 \implies \boxed{P=₱28} \]
Final Answer:

The price associated with supplying 40 units is ₱28 per unit.

Summary of demand and supply

Characteristic Demand (\(D\)) Supply (\(S\))
Perspective Represents customers' willingness to buy Represents sellers' willingness to sell
Behavior with Price Usually decreases as price increases Usually increases as price increases
Curve Slope Commonly slopes downward (\(\searrow\)) Commonly slopes upward (\(\nearrow\))
Linear Formula \(Q_d=a-bP\) \(Q_s=c+dP\)
Part 6

Market Equilibrium

18. What is market equilibrium?

Market equilibrium occurs when the quantity demanded is equal to the quantity supplied.

In simpler words, it is the point where the amount customers want to buy matches the amount businesses want to sell.

\[ \boxed{Q_d=Q_s} \]

The corresponding price is called the equilibrium price (\(P_e\)), and the corresponding quantity is called the equilibrium quantity (\(Q_e\)).

19. Solving linear market equilibrium

Worked Example 11

Market for School Bags

Suppose the demand and supply functions for school bags are:

  • Demand: \[ Q_d=200-4P \]
  • Supply: \[ Q_s=20+3P \]

Question: Find the equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity.

Step 1
Understand the equilibrium condition: At equilibrium, demand equals supply: \[ Q_d=Q_s \]
Step 2
Substitute the two functions: \[ 200-4P=20+3P \]
Step 3
Move the numbers to one side: Subtract 20 from both sides: \[ 180-4P=3P \]
Step 4
Move the price terms to one side: Add \(4P\) to both sides: \[ 180=7P \]
Step 5
Divide both sides by 7: \[ P=\frac{180}{7} \implies \boxed{P\approx ₱25.71} \] This is the equilibrium price.
Step 6
Find the equilibrium quantity: Substitute the price into either the demand or supply function.
Using demand: \[ Q_d=200-4(25.7143) \] \[ Q_d\approx 200-102.857 \implies \boxed{Q\approx 97.14} \]
Final Answer:

Equilibrium price: approximately ₱25.71

Equilibrium quantity: approximately 97.14 units

In an actual market selling whole school bags, the practical quantity would need to be a whole number (e.g. 97 bags).

20. Solving nonlinear market equilibrium

Worked Example 12

Nonlinear Demand and Supply

Suppose the market follows these functions:

  • Demand: \[ P_D=120-0.01Q^2 \]
  • Supply: \[ P_S=20+0.005Q^2 \]

Question: Find the equilibrium price and quantity.

Step 1
Set demand equal to supply: \[ 120-0.01Q^2=20+0.005Q^2 \]
Step 2
Move the constant numbers to one side: \[ 120-20=0.01Q^2+0.005Q^2 \]
Step 3
Combine similar terms: \[ 100=0.015Q^2 \]
Step 4
Divide by 0.015: \[ Q^2=\frac{100}{0.015}\approx 6,666.67 \]
Step 5
Get the square root: \[ Q=\sqrt{6,666.67} \] Because quantity cannot be negative in this business example: \[ \boxed{Q\approx 81.65} \]
Step 6
Find the equilibrium price: Substitute \(Q\) into the supply function: \[ P_S=20+0.005Q^2 \] \[ P_S=20+0.005(6,666.67) \] \[ P_S=20+33.33 \implies \boxed{P\approx ₱53.33} \]
Final Answer:

The equilibrium quantity is approximately 81.65 units, and the equilibrium price is approximately ₱53.33.

Remember: Market equilibrium is important because it helps businesses understand the price and quantity at which the market's demand and supply match.
Part 7

Break-Even Analysis

21. What is break-even analysis?

Break-even analysis is a business method used to determine how many products a company must sell to cover all its costs.

Total revenue equals total cost.
The business does not earn a profit (\(\pi = 0\)).
The business does not experience a loss.

In other words, the business earns exactly enough revenue to pay its expenses.

\[ \boxed{R(x)=C(x)} \]
Reference: Break-Even Point Formula: Analysis & Examples - Invoice Fly (invoicefly.com)

Understanding break-even using a simple example

Imagine that you start a small burger business. Your expenses are:

  • Monthly rent: ₱12,000
  • Cost to make each burger: ₱45
  • Selling price of each burger: ₱75

You need to determine how many burgers you must sell before earning a profit.

The amount left from each burger sale after paying its ₱45 production cost is:

\[ 75-45=₱30 \]

This ₱30 is called the contribution margin per unit. It helps pay the fixed costs first. After all fixed costs are covered, additional contribution becomes profit under this simple model.

22. Step-by-step linear break-even analysis

Worked Example 13

Burger Business Break-Even Calculation

Given:

Business informationAmount
Fixed cost (\(F\))₱12,000
Variable cost per burger (\(v\))₱45
Selling price per burger (\(p\))₱75

Question: How many burgers must the business sell to break even?

Step 1
Write the revenue function: Revenue is selling price multiplied by quantity: \[ R(x)=75x \]
Step 2
Write the cost function: Total cost equals fixed cost plus variable cost: \[ C(x)=12,000+45x \]
Step 3
Set revenue equal to cost: \[ R(x)=C(x) \] \[ 75x=12,000+45x \]
Step 4
Move the variable terms to one side: Subtract \(45x\) from both sides: \[ 75x-45x=12,000 \]
Step 5
Subtract: \[ 30x=12,000 \]
Step 6
Divide by 30: \[ x=\frac{12,000}{30} \implies \boxed{x=400} \]
Final Answer:

The business must sell 400 burgers to break even.

Step 7: Verify the answer

Calculate revenue at 400 units: \[ R(400)=75(400)=₱30,000 \]

Calculate total cost at 400 units: \[ C(400)=12,000+45(400)=12,000+18,000=₱30,000 \]

Both revenue and cost equal ₱30,000. Therefore, 400 burgers is the correct break-even quantity.

Step 8: What happens after break-even?

Let's compare different sales quantities:

  • Selling exactly 400 burgers means breaking even.
  • Selling more than 400 burgers produces a profit.

23. Nonlinear break-even analysis

Some businesses have nonlinear revenue or cost functions. They may have more than one break-even point.

Worked Example 14

Product with Changing Selling Prices

A company has the following price function: \[ p(x)=120-x \]

Its cost function is: \[ C(x)=1,600+20x \]

Question: Find the break-even quantities.

Step 1
Construct the revenue function: \[ R(x)=xp(x)=x(120-x)=120x-x^2 \]
Step 2
Set revenue equal to cost: \[ 120x-x^2=1,600+20x \]
Step 3
Move all terms to one side: \[ 120x-x^2-1,600-20x=0 \]
Step 4
Combine similar terms and multiply by -1: \[ -x^2+100x-1,600=0 \] Multiply both sides by -1: \[ x^2-100x+1,600=0 \]
Step 5
Factor the quadratic equation: We need two numbers that multiply to 1,600 and add to -100. Those numbers are -20 and -80: \[ (x-20)(x-80)=0 \]
Step 6
Solve each factor:
First: \(x-20=0 \implies x=20\)
Second: \(x-80=0 \implies x=80\)
Final Answer:

The company has two break-even quantities: \[ \boxed{x=20 \text{ and } x=80} \]

What does this mean?

The company breaks even at 20 units and again at 80 units.

Between these quantities (from \(20 < x < 80\)), the mathematical model predicts a profit. For example, at 50 units: \(\pi(50)=₱900\).

Beyond 80 units, the price has dropped so low to sell high volume that revenue once again drops below costs.

Important business lesson: A company should not always assume that producing and selling more products will continuously increase profit. Selling prices, demand, and production costs can change.
Part 8

Introduction to Limits

24. What is a limit?

A limit describes the value that a function approaches as its input approaches a particular number.

In simpler words, a limit answers this question: What value is a function getting closer to when the input gets closer to a certain number?

Limits are one of the most important foundations of calculus.

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x) \]
Read as: "The limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\)."
  • \(x\) = input or quantity
  • \(a\) = number that \(x\) is approaching
  • \(f(x)\) = output or business result

Simple everyday example

Imagine a business gradually increasing its production from 90 units to 100 units. We want to study what happens to its average cost as production approaches 100 units.

The production quantities might be: 99, 99.5, 99.9, 99.99, and so on. These values are getting closer to 100. A limit allows us to study the value the average cost approaches as production approaches 100.

Important idea: A limit is concerned with what happens near a particular value, not necessarily what happens exactly at that value.

25. Understanding a limit through a table

Suppose a business uses the function: \[ f(x)=2x+5 \]

Find the limit as \(x\) approaches 3: \[ \lim_{x\to3}(2x+5) \]

Let's observe what happens when \(x\) gets closer to 3 from both sides:

Direction \(x\) \(f(x)=2x+5\) Observation
Approaching from Left2.09Approaching 11 from below \(\nearrow\)
Approaching from Left2.510
Approaching from Left2.910.8
Approaching from Left2.9910.98
Exact Target3.011Value = 11
Approaching from Right3.0111.02Approaching 11 from above \(\searrow\)
Approaching from Right3.111.2
Approaching from Right3.512

Notice that when \(x\) gets closer to 3 from either direction, the function gets closer to 11.

\[ \boxed{\lim_{x\to3}(2x+5)=11} \]

Why is the answer 11? Because the function's output approaches 11 when the input approaches 3.

Evaluating Limits

26. Direct substitution method

The easiest way to evaluate many limits is through direct substitution. This means replacing the variable with the number it is approaching.

Worked Example 15

Basic Limit

Evaluate: \[ \lim_{x\to4}(3x+7) \]

1
Identify approaching value: \(x\to4\)
2
Substitute 4 for \(x\): \(3(4)+7\)
3
Multiply: \(12+7\)
4
Add: \(\boxed{19}\)

The limit is 19.

Worked Example 16

Polynomial Limit

Evaluate: \[ \lim_{x\to3}(x^2+2x+4) \]

1
Substitute 3 for \(x\): \((3)^2+2(3)+4\)
2
Apply exponent: \(9+6+4\)
3
Add: \(\boxed{19}\)

The limit is 19.

Business Application

Total Production Cost Limit

A company uses this cost function: \[ C(x)=2,000+15x \]

Determine the cost as production approaches 100 units: \[ \lim_{x\to100}(2,000+15x) \]

Step 1: Substitute 100: \(C(100)=2,000+15(100)\)

Step 2: Multiply: \(C(100)=2,000+1,500\)

\[ \boxed{₱3,500} \]

The cost approaches ₱3,500 as production approaches 100 units.

27. Limits involving fractions

Direct substitution also works for many rational functions, provided the denominator does not become zero.

Worked Example 17

Rational Limit

Evaluate: \[ \lim_{x\to2}\frac{x+6}{x+1} \]

Step 1
Substitute 2 into numerator & denominator: \(\frac{2+6}{2+1}\)
Step 2
Simplify the numerator and denominator: \(\frac{8}{3}\)
Final Answer:

\[ \boxed{\frac{8}{3}} \approx 2.67 \]

28. Limits requiring factoring

Sometimes direct substitution produces: \[ \frac{0}{0} \] This is called an indeterminate form. It does not mean the limit is zero or that the limit does not exist. We may need to simplify the expression first.

Worked Example 18

Factoring Method

Evaluate: \[ \lim_{x\to3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} \]

Step 1
Try direct substitution: \[ \frac{3^2-9}{3-3}=\frac{9-9}{0}=\frac{0}{0} \] Direct substitution does not give the answer!
Step 2
Factor the numerator: Remember the difference of two squares: \[ a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b) \] Therefore: \[ x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3) \] Rewrite the expression: \[ \lim_{x\to3}\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} \]
Step 3
Cancel the common factor: For values near 3 but not equal to 3: \[ = x+3 \]
Step 4
Substitute 3: \[ 3+3=6 \implies \boxed{6} \]
Final Answer:

The limit is 6.

Why does this work?

The original expression is undefined at \(x=3\), but nearby values can still approach a definite number. The limit is about the values near 3, so the expression can still have a limit even though it is not defined at 3.

29. Another example of factoring

Example

Factoring with Difference of Squares

Evaluate: \[ \lim_{x\to5}\frac{x^2-25}{x-5} \]

Step 1
Try direct substitution: \(\frac{5^2-25}{5-5}=\frac{0}{0}\) (indeterminate)
Step 2
Factor numerator: \(x^2-25=(x-5)(x+5)\)
Step 3
Cancel common factor \((x-5)\): \(=x+5\)
Step 4
Substitute 5: \(5+5=10 \implies \boxed{10}\)

The limit is 10.

30. One-sided limits

A limit can approach a number from either the left or the right.

Left-hand limit

The input approaches a number using values smaller than that number.

\[ \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x) \]

Right-hand limit

The input approaches a number using values greater than that number.

\[ \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x) \]

Condition for existence: For an ordinary two-sided limit to exist, the left-hand and right-hand limits must be equal: \[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x\to a^-}f(x) = \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x) = L \]

Worked Example 19

One-Sided Limits in Delivery Service Fees

Suppose a delivery service charges:

\[ f(x)= \begin{cases} 100, & x<10\\ 150, & x\geq10 \end{cases} \]

Here, \(x\) represents the number of items ordered and \(f(x)\) represents a fee.

Question: Find the left-hand and right-hand limits as \(x\) approaches 10.

Step 1
Examine values from the left (\(x < 10\)): When \(x < 10\), the fee is ₱100: \[ \lim_{x\to10^-}f(x)=100 \]
Step 2
Examine values from the right (\(x \ge 10\)): When \(x \ge 10\), the fee is ₱150: \[ \lim_{x\to10^+}f(x)=150 \]
Step 3
Compare the two limits: \[ 100 \ne 150 \]
Final Answer:

The two-sided limit does not exist because the left-hand and right-hand limits are different.

This situation can occur in business models involving fees, price changes, and promotional thresholds.

31. Limits at infinity

A limit can also describe what happens to a function when the input becomes extremely large:

\[ \lim_{x\to\infty}f(x) \]

This asks what value the function approaches as \(x\) increases without bound.

Business Example

Average Cost as Production Grows

A company has the following average cost function: \[ AC(x)=20+\frac{3,000}{x} \]

Here:

  • ₱20 represents the per-unit variable cost.
  • ₱3,000 represents the fixed cost spread across the production quantity.

Let's calculate the average cost at different quantities:

Quantity (\(x\)) Average cost per unit (\(AC(x)\))
100₱50
200₱35
500₱26
1,000₱23
3,000₱21

As production increases, the fixed cost is spread across more products. Therefore, average cost gets closer to ₱20.

\[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\left(20+\frac{3,000}{x}\right)=20 \]
Business interpretation: Under this simplified cost model, the average cost approaches ₱20 per unit as production becomes very large (economies of scale).
Part 10

Continuity in Business Models

32. What is continuity?

A function is continuous when its graph has no break, jump, or hole at the point being examined.

In simple words, a continuous function changes without an unexpected break at that point.

The Pencil Intuition: Think of drawing a graph using a pencil. If you can draw the graph around a point without lifting your pencil, that is an intuitive picture of continuity.

Continuity is useful in business because many business models represent gradual changes in costs, prices, production, and revenue.

Examples of continuous business relationships:
Production cost increasing as more products are produced Revenue increasing as more products are sold at a constant price Delivery cost increasing gradually with distance A service fee increasing with additional usage

33. Three conditions for continuity

A function \(f(x)\) is continuous at \(x=a\) if all three conditions are satisfied:

Condition 1

Defined at Point

\[ f(a)\text{ exists} \]

The function has an actual output value at \(x=a\) (no division by zero or empty hole).

Condition 2

Limit Exists

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)\text{ exists} \]

The left-hand and right-hand limits approach the exact same finite value.

Condition 3

Limit Equals Value

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) \]

The value the function approaches matches the actual value at that point.

All three conditions must be satisfied!

34. Worked Example 20: Continuous business function

Worked Example 20

Checking Continuity of a Cost Function

A company uses this cost function: \[ C(x)=500+20x \]

Question: Determine whether the function is continuous at \(x=10\).

Step 1
Find the function's value: Substitute 10: \[ C(10)=500+20(10)=500+200=700 \] Condition 1 satisfied: \(C(10)=700\) exists.
Step 2
Find the limit as \(x\) approaches 10: \[ \lim_{x\to10}(500+20x)=500+20(10)=700 \] Condition 2 satisfied: Limit exists and equals 700.
Step 3
Compare the limit and function value: \[ \lim_{x\to10}C(x)=C(10) \] \[ 700=700 \] Condition 3 satisfied: Both are equal!
Conclusion: Continuous

The cost function is continuous at \(x=10\) because its limit exists and equals its actual function value.

35. Continuity in piecewise business functions

Some business functions use different formulas depending on the quantity or price. These are called piecewise functions.

For example, a subscription company offers a plan that costs ₱500 for the first 100 units of usage. After 100 units, customers pay an additional ₱5 for every extra unit.

\[ F(x)= \begin{cases} 500, & x\leq100\\ 500+5(x-100), & x>100 \end{cases} \]
\(x\) = units of usage • \(F(x)\) = total fee

Is the function continuous at 100 units?

Step 1
Find the left-hand limit: For values at or below 100, the fee is ₱500: \[ \lim_{x\to100^-}F(x)=500 \]
Step 2
Find the right-hand limit: For values above 100, use \(F(x)=500+5(x-100)\): \[ \lim_{x\to100^+}F(x)=500+5(100-100)=500+0=500 \]
Step 3
Compare one-sided limits: \(500=500\), so the limit exists and equals 500.
Step 4
Check actual function value: Because \(100\leq100\), use the first piece: \(F(100)=500\). The limit and actual value are equal!
Conclusion: Continuous

The subscription fee is continuous at 100 units.

The rate of increase changes at 100 units, but the fee itself does not suddenly jump. A continuous function does not have to be a straight line.

36. Discontinuity in business models

A function is discontinuous when there is a break, jump, or hole in its graph.

When does discontinuity happen in business?
  • A company suddenly changes its prices.
  • A promotional fee is removed at a specific quantity.
  • A customer moves into a different pricing bracket.
  • A business imposes a sudden additional charge.
Worked Example 21

Discontinuous Pricing Function

A company charges:

  • ₱100 for every unit when fewer than 10 units are ordered.
  • ₱90 for every unit when 10 or more units are ordered.

The total cost is represented by:

\[ C(x)= \begin{cases} 100x, & x<10\\ 90x, & x\geq10 \end{cases} \]

Question: Determine whether the function is continuous at \(x=10\).

Step 1
Find the left-hand limit: When approaching 10 from the left (\(x<10\)): \[ \lim_{x\to10^-}C(x)=100(10)=1,000 \]
Step 2
Find the right-hand limit: When approaching 10 from the right (\(x\geq10\)): \[ \lim_{x\to10^+}C(x)=90(10)=900 \]
Step 3
Compare the limits: \[ 1,000\ne900 \] The left-hand and right-hand limits are different. Therefore, the limit does not exist at \(x=10\).
Conclusion: Discontinuous

The function is discontinuous at \(x=10\).

Business interpretation

The total price suddenly drops from nearly ₱1,000 (e.g. ₱999 for 9.99 units or ₱900 for 9 units at ₱100) to ₱900 when the order reaches 10 units because the discount applies to all units. This creates a jump in the cost function.

Important: A business model can be discontinuous even when the business itself operates normally. The discontinuity is a mathematical feature of the pricing rule.

A. Business functions

Problem 1

Linear cost

A business has a fixed cost of ₱8,000 and a variable cost of ₱25 per product.

  1. Construct the cost function.
  2. Find the total cost of producing 100 products.
Solution 1: Linear cost

Given: \[ F=8,000,\quad v=25 \]

a. Cost function: \[ C(x)=8,000+25x \]

b. Substitute 100:

\[ C(100)=8,000+25(100) = 8,000+2,500 = \boxed{₱10,500} \]

Problem 2

Nonlinear cost

A company has the cost function: \[ C(x)=1,000+10x+0.1x^2 \]

Find the cost of producing 20 units.

Solution 2: Nonlinear cost

Given: \[ C(x)=1,000+10x+0.1x^2 \]

Substitute 20:

\[ C(20)=1,000+10(20)+0.1(20)^2 = 1,000+200+0.1(400) = 1,000+200+40 = \boxed{₱1,240} \]

Problem 3

Linear revenue

A store sells bags for ₱450 each. Find its revenue if it sells 30 bags.

Solution 3: Linear revenue

\[ R(x)=450x \]

\[ R(30)=450(30) = \boxed{₱13,500} \]

Problem 4

Nonlinear revenue

A company uses the price function: \[ p(x)=100-2x \]

Find the revenue if it sells 20 products.

Solution 4: Nonlinear revenue

Given: \[ p(x)=100-2x \]

First, find the price:

\[ p(20)=100-2(20)=100-40=60 \]

Then calculate revenue:

\[ R(20)=20(60) = \boxed{₱1,200} \]

Problem 5

Profit

A company has:

\[ R(x)=100x \]

\[ C(x)=2,000+40x \]

Find the profit function and calculate the profit when 50 products are sold.

Solution 5: Profit

Given: \[ R(x)=100x,\quad C(x)=2,000+40x \]

Apply the profit formula:

\[ \pi(x)=R(x)-C(x) = 100x-(2,000+40x) = 100x-2,000-40x \]

\[ \boxed{\pi(x)=60x-2,000} \]

Now substitute 50:

\[ \pi(50)=60(50)-2,000 = 3,000-2,000 = \boxed{₱1,000} \]

B. Demand and supply

Problem 6

Demand

A product has the demand function: \[ Q_d=150-5P \]

Find the quantity demanded when the price is ₱20.

Solution 6: Demand

\[ Q_d=150-5P \]

Substitute \(P=20\):

\[ Q_d=150-5(20) = 150-100 = \boxed{50\text{ units}} \]

Problem 7

Supply

A supplier has the supply function: \[ Q_s=10+4P \]

Find the quantity supplied when the price is ₱15.

Solution 7: Supply

\[ Q_s=10+4P \]

Substitute \(P=15\):

\[ Q_s=10+4(15) = 10+60 = \boxed{70\text{ units}} \]

Problem 8

Market equilibrium

Given: \[ Q_d=120-2P \] \[ Q_s=20+3P \]

Find the equilibrium price and quantity.

Solution 8: Market equilibrium

Given: \[ 120-2P=20+3P \]

Move the terms:

\[ 120-20=3P+2P \]

\[ 100=5P \implies \boxed{P=20} \]

Substitute into the demand function:

\[ Q_d=120-2(20)=120-40 \implies \boxed{Q=80} \]

The equilibrium price is ₱20 and the equilibrium quantity is 80 units.

C. Break-even analysis

Problem 9

Break-even

A business has:

  • Fixed cost: ₱15,000
  • Variable cost: ₱50 per unit
  • Selling price: ₱100 per unit

Determine the break-even quantity.

Solution 9: Break-even

Given: \[ F=15,000,\quad v=50,\quad p=100 \]

Revenue: \[ R(x)=100x \]

Cost: \[ C(x)=15,000+50x \]

Set revenue equal to cost:

\[ 100x=15,000+50x \]

Subtract \(50x\):

\[ 50x=15,000 \]

Divide by 50: \[ \boxed{x=300} \]

The business must sell 300 units to break even.

D. Limits and continuity

Problem 10

Direct substitution

Evaluate: \[ \lim_{x\to4}(2x+6) \]

Solution 10: Direct substitution

\[ \lim_{x\to4}(2x+6) \]

Substitute 4: \[ =2(4)+6 = 8+6 = \boxed{14} \]

Problem 11

Polynomial limit

Evaluate: \[ \lim_{x\to2}(x^2+3x+1) \]

Solution 11: Polynomial limit

\[ \lim_{x\to2}(x^2+3x+1) \]

Substitute 2: \[ =(2)^2+3(2)+1 = 4+6+1 = \boxed{11} \]

Problem 12

Factoring

Evaluate: \[ \lim_{x\to4}\frac{x^2-16}{x-4} \]

Solution 12: Factoring

\[ \lim_{x\to4}\frac{x^2-16}{x-4} \]

Factor the numerator: \[ =\lim_{x\to4}\frac{(x-4)(x+4)}{x-4} \]

Cancel the common factor: \[ =\lim_{x\to4}(x+4) \]

Substitute 4: \[ =4+4 = \boxed{8} \]

Problem 13

Continuity

Given: \[ f(x)=3x+5 \]

Determine whether the function is continuous at \(x=2\).

Solution 13: Continuity

Given: \[ f(x)=3x+5 \]

Find the function value: \[ f(2)=3(2)+5=11 \]

Find the limit: \[ \lim_{x\to2}(3x+5)=11 \]

Compare: \[ \lim_{x\to2}f(x)=f(2) \]

Both equal 11.

\[ \boxed{\text{The function is continuous at }x=2.} \]

Problem 14

Discontinuity

Given:

\[ f(x)= \begin{cases} 50, & x<5\\ 80, & x\geq5 \end{cases} \]

Determine whether the function is continuous at \(x=5\).

Solution 14: Discontinuity

Left-hand limit: \[ \lim_{x\to5^-}f(x)=50 \]

Right-hand limit: \[ \lim_{x\to5^+}f(x)=80 \]

Because: \[ 50\ne80 \]

The two-sided limit does not exist.

\[ \boxed{\text{The function is discontinuous at }x=5.} \]

1

Business functions

Cost functions

Limits

A limit tells us the value that a function approaches as its input approaches a particular number.

1. COST STRUCTURE

\[ C(x) = F + vx \]
Fixed Cost (\(F\)): Rent, permits, insurance (constant regardless of output).
Variable Cost (\(vx\)): Materials, packaging, labor (\(v \times x\)).
Nonlinear Model: \(C(x) = F + ax + bx^2\) (bulk discounts, overtime fatigue).

2. REVENUE DYNAMICS

\[ R(x) = px \]
Linear: Constant price \(p\) per unit sold.
Nonlinear: Price drops as volume rises: \[ p(x) = a - bx \implies R(x) = ax - bx^2 \]
Peak revenue occurs at vertex before price discounts reduce gains.

3. PROFIT & BREAK-EVEN

\[ \pi(x) = R(x) - C(x) \] \[ Q^* = \frac{F}{p - v} \]
Break-Even: \(R(x) = C(x) \implies \pi(x) = 0\).
Contribution Margin: \(p - v\) pays off fixed costs first.
\(x < Q^* \to\) Loss • \(x > Q^* \to\) Profit

4. MARKET EQUILIBRIUM

\[ Q_d = Q_s \]
Demand (\(Q_d = a - bP\)): Slopes downward (\(P \uparrow \implies Q_d \downarrow\)).
Supply (\(Q_s = c + dP\)): Slopes upward (\(P \uparrow \implies Q_s \uparrow\)).
Equilibrium point \((Q_e, P_e)\) clears the market with no shortage or surplus.

5. LIMITS IN BUSINESS

\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \]
Concept: Value approached as input gets infinitesimally close.
Direct Substitution: Plug in \(a\).
If \(\frac{0}{0}\) (Indeterminate): Factor, cancel, and re-substitute!
At Infinity: Fixed costs dilute: \(\lim_{x \to \infty} (20 + \frac{3000}{x}) = ₱20\).

6. CONTINUITY CHECKLIST

\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a) \]
1. \(f(a)\) exists (defined, no hole)
2. \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\) exists (Left = Right)
3. Limit = Value (seamless graph)
Discontinuous: Wholesale price tiers with sudden jumps.
